## Headliners

### Anthropic Accuses Alibaba of Largest Known Distillation Attack — 28.8 Million Exchanges, 25,000 Fraudulent Accounts, Legislative Action Underway

**Anthropic has formally accused Chinese tech giant Alibaba of orchestrating the largest known AI model distillation attack in history, alleging that operators affiliated with Alibaba and its AI research division Alibaba Qwen conducted a coordinated campaign generating more than 28.8 million exchanges with Claude through nearly 25,000 fraudulent accounts between April 22 and June 5, 2026 — and the disclosure is already driving bipartisan legislative action on Capitol Hill.** The accusation, detailed in a June 10 letter to Senate Banking Committee leadership and first reported by Bloomberg on June 24, represents the most significant escalation yet in the confrontation over AI model theft between US and Chinese AI sectors. ([Cybersecurity News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/anthropic-accuses-alibaba/) | [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/anthropic-says-alibaba-illicitly-extracted-claude-ai-model-capabilities-2026-06-24/) | [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-24/anthropic-accuses-alibaba-of-illicitly-accessing-its-ai-models))

**The attack mechanics.** The campaign specifically targeted Claude's most advanced and commercially valuable capabilities — software engineering and agentic reasoning, the cornerstones of Anthropic's cutting-edge Mythos Preview model. The attack relied on "adversarial distillation," a technique where a less capable AI model is trained on the outputs of a more powerful one to mimic its capabilities at a fraction of the development cost. Anthropic wrote in its letter: "These distillation attacks are carried out illicitly, systematically, and at an industrial scale to harvest U.S. AI capabilities across frontier labs and repackage them as their own without incurring the training and R&D costs required to train U.S. frontier models."

**The safety implications.** Anthropic further warned that AI systems built through adversarial distillation often lack safety guardrails, posing broader security and safety risks beyond intellectual property theft. A distilled model that replicates Claude's software engineering capabilities without its safety training is a model that can be used for offensive purposes without the constraints that Anthropic builds into its own deployments.

**The legislative response.** Senators Bill Hagerty (R-TN) and Andy Kim (D-NJ) are moving to introduce an amendment to must-pass defense legislation that would blacklist or sanction any Chinese firm found improperly accessing US AI model outputs to train competing systems. The bipartisan amendment signals that AI model theft is being treated as a national security issue requiring statutory response — not just corporate legal action.

**The pattern.** This is not an isolated incident. In February 2026, Anthropic revealed a separate scheme involving DeepSeek and two other Chinese AI laboratories attempting to illicitly access Claude's platform. The company now describes this as a "systematic and unauthorized" pattern of exploitation of leading US AI models to build a rival generation of Chinese chatbots. The disclosure arrives just weeks after the Trump administration ordered Anthropic to suspend access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all foreign nationals — a directive that has now blocked even US intelligence agencies from using the models.

**Why this is a Headliner:** The Alibaba distillation attack is the largest known incident of its kind and represents a structural threat to the US AI industry's competitive advantage. The legislative response — sanctions on Chinese firms found stealing AI model outputs — would create a new regulatory framework for AI model security that goes beyond corporate terms of service enforcement. For security teams, the incident underscores that AI model access controls are now a national security concern, and that distillation attacks are being conducted at industrial scale — not as one-off experiments but as systematic intelligence-gathering operations.

## Breaking News

### DifyTap: Critical Cross-Tenant Data Exposure in Dify AI Platform — CVSS 9.1 and 9.4, 1M+ Applications Impacted

**Security researchers at Zafran have disclosed four critical vulnerabilities in the Dify AI platform — including CVE-2026-41947 (CVSS 9.1) and CVE-2026-41948 (CVSS 9.4) — that enable cross-tenant data exposure, allowing attackers to silently wiretap AI conversations, access sensitive files, and exfiltrate full chat histories including prompts and model responses across tenant boundaries, impacting more than one million applications.** Dify, which powers AI workflows, chatbots, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines, is heavily adopted across enterprises including Volvo, Maersk, Panasonic, and Thermo Fisher, with over 140,000 GitHub stars and more than 10 million Docker pulls. ([Cybersecurity News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/difytap-flaws-wiretap-ai-data-across-tenants/) | [Zafran](https://www.zafran.io/resources/difytap-zafran-discovers-how-attackers-can-silently-wiretap-ai-data-across-tenants-on-a-platform-powering-1m-apps))

**The attack surface.** Zafran identified tens of thousands of internet-facing Dify instances during its investigation. The most severe issue allows attackers to configure tracing on victim applications without proper tenant validation — effectively creating a persistent data exfiltration channel that captures every prompt and model response. Additional vulnerabilities include path traversal flaws in Dify's Plugin Daemon service (no authentication required), unauthorized file preview across tenants using only file UUIDs, and the ability to attach existing file identifiers to new messages to trick AI models into revealing file contents.

**The dependency risk.** Beyond the logic flaws, Dify was found to be using an outdated version of PDFium vulnerable to CVE-2024-5846, a use-after-free bug that remained in production for over 18 months after disclosure. This highlights a broader issue in AI platforms that process untrusted file formats without adequate sandboxing or dependency management.

**The fix.** Dify has released version 1.14.2, addressing CVE-2026-41947, CVE-2026-41949, and CVE-2026-41950. A fix for CVE-2026-41948 has been merged and is expected in an upcoming release. Security teams should immediately upgrade, deploy WAF rules to block path traversal attacks, and limit public exposure of Dify instances.

**Why it matters:** DifyTap is the most concrete demonstration yet that AI application platforms — not just the models themselves — are accumulating critical vulnerabilities at scale. The cross-tenant data exposure means that organizations running Dify in multi-tenant configurations may have had their AI conversations, including proprietary business data and customer information, exposed to other tenants. For security teams, the incident underscores that AI platform security must include tenant isolation validation, dependency auditing, and file-handling sandboxing — not just model-level controls.

### Cordyceps: New CI/CD Weakness Class Exposes 300+ GitHub Repositories — Microsoft, Google, Apache, Cloudflare Affected

**Security researchers at Novee Security have disclosed "Cordyceps," a new class of CI/CD workflow weakness that allows any unauthenticated user to hijack workflows and compromise open-source supply chains — affecting more than 300 high-impact GitHub repositories including those belonging to Microsoft, Google, Apache, and Cloudflare, with the vulnerability lying not in any single component but in the composition of untrusted data crossing trust boundaries that no one audited.** Novee's scan of approximately 30,000 high-impact repositories revealed more than 300 to be fully exploitable, enabling attacker-controlled code execution, credential theft, and supply chain compromise. ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/06/cordyceps-cicd-flaws-expose-300-github.html) | [Novee Security](https://novee.security/blog/cordyceps/) | [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/exploitable-ci-cd-vulnerabilities-expose-millions-of-repositories-to-hijacking/))

**The mechanics.** The core problem is weak CI/CD configurations that grant pull requests (PRs) more permissions than they should have. Because an untrusted PR can trigger privileged workflows, it opens the door to command injection, privilege escalation, and supply chain compromise. As Novee's founding engineer Elad Meged explained: "The flaw is exploitable by any unauthenticated user. No org membership or special privileges; a free account is enough to forge approvals, push code, or steal credentials."

**Specific findings.** On Microsoft's Azure Sentinel, a comment on a PR could run anonymous attacker code on Microsoft's CI and steal a non-expiring GitHub App key. On Google's AI Agent Development Kit ("adk-samples"), a PR could execute attacker code on Google's CI to gain complete authority over a Google Cloud repository. Apache Doris had two zero-click attacks where a single comment on any PR could exfiltrate hard-coded CI credentials. Cloudflare Workers SDK had a PR with a crafted branch name that could execute arbitrary commands on Cloudflare's CI runners. The Python Software Foundation's Black formatter had a single PR from anyone that could execute attacker code on Black's build systems.

**The AI connection.** Novee specifically noted that "the nature of agentic coding means these CI/CD vulnerabilities are reproduced persistently, at scale, 'infecting' repositories at an exponential rate." AI coding agents that generate PRs and workflows are inadvertently reproducing the same insecure patterns across thousands of repositories.

**Why it matters:** Cordyceps is not a vulnerability in any single product — it is a structural weakness in how CI/CD pipelines handle untrusted input across trust boundaries. The fact that it affects Microsoft, Google, Apache, and Cloudflare simultaneously demonstrates that this is not a configuration error by a single organization but a systemic pattern. For security teams, the implication is that CI/CD pipeline security needs to be audited for trust boundary violations — not just for known CVEs — and that AI-generated workflows need automated security validation before deployment.

### Edgecution: Malicious Edge Extension Escapes Browser Sandbox — Deploys Python Backdoor for Ransomware

**Security researchers at Zscaler ThreatLabz have uncovered "Edgecution," a malicious Microsoft Edge extension used in an active ransomware attack campaign that abuses the Chrome native messaging protocol to escape the browser sandbox and deploy a Python-based backdoor on the victim's system — distributed through fake IT Teams messages masquerading as Outlook update notifications.** The campaign, attributed to an initial access broker known as "Payouts King," represents a significant escalation in browser-based attack techniques. ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/malicious-edge-extension-abuses-native-messaging-as-bridge-to-malware/) | [GBHackers](https://gbhackers.com/payouts-king-initial-access-broker/) | [Cybersecurity News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/malicious-edge-extension-uses-chrome-native-messaging/))

**The attack chain.** The campaign begins with a phishing message impersonating IT support, directing the target to a fake Outlook update portal. The portal prompts the user to install a browser extension that appears legitimate but is actually the Edgecution malware. Once installed, the extension abuses the Chrome native messaging protocol — a legitimate API that allows extensions to communicate with native applications — to reach a Python backdoor already running on the endpoint. This effectively escapes the browser sandbox and gives the attacker full system access, enabling ransomware deployment.

**The AI relevance.** While not an AI-specific attack, Edgecution is directly relevant to the AI security landscape for two reasons. First, AI coding agents increasingly use browser automation — and a compromised browser is a compromised agent environment. Second, the native messaging abuse technique is the same class of trust boundary violation that MCP-based attacks exploit: a legitimate protocol bridge between a sandboxed environment and native system access.

**Why it matters:** Edgecution demonstrates that browser extensions remain a potent initial access vector, and that native messaging protocols — designed for legitimate use cases — can be weaponized to bypass sandbox protections. For organizations deploying AI agents that use browser automation, the attack provides a concrete example of why browser security matters for AI security: if the browser is compromised, the agent operating through it is compromised too.

([NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/23/business/meta-ai-government-reviews-security.html) | [TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/us-presses-meta-ai-security-reviews) | [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2200490/us-government-reportedly-urging-meta-to-share-its-ai-models/))

**The context.** The June 2 executive order created a voluntary review structure for frontier AI models. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft have all agreed to participate. Meta's refusal puts the company in a politically exposed position, particularly given the administration's demonstrated willingness to use export controls — as it did with Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 on June 12 — when it perceives national security risks from AI model capabilities.

Meta's argument against participation has centered on the open-source nature of its models — but the administration's position is that open-weight distribution makes security review more important, not less.

**Why it matters:** Meta's holdout status creates a two-tier system where some frontier AI models undergo federal security review and others do not. For enterprise customers evaluating AI platforms, the question of whether a model has undergone federal review may become a procurement requirement — particularly for government contractors and regulated industries. The August 1 framework deadline means this issue will come to a head within 37 days.

## Technical Deep Dives

### SecurityWeek's New AI Supply Chain Risk Prioritization Framework: When Every Vulnerability Is Critical, Which Ones Matter Most?

**SecurityWeek has published a new framework designed to help security teams identify which software supply chain vulnerabilities pose the greatest operational, safety, and business risks in AI-driven environments — addressing the growing crisis where AI-powered vulnerability discovery is producing findings at a rate that exceeds the remediation pipeline's capacity, and organizations need a systematic way to triage what matters most.** The framework, published June 24-25, arrives at a moment when OpenAI's Daybreak and Anthropic's Glasswing are finding vulnerabilities in infrastructure software at a pace that makes traditional CVSS-based prioritization inadequate. ([SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/))

**The prioritization problem.** Traditional vulnerability prioritization relies on CVSS scores, which measure technical severity in isolation — how bad the bug is if exploited. But in an AI-driven development environment where thousands of vulnerabilities may be discovered in a single codebase scan, technical severity alone is insufficient. The SecurityWeek framework adds three additional dimensions: operational impact (what systems would be affected if exploited), safety risk (whether the vulnerability could cause physical harm or regulatory violation), and business risk (whether the vulnerable component is in a revenue-critical or compliance-critical path).

**The AI-specific dimension.** The framework specifically addresses how AI-generated code introduces vulnerabilities differently from human-written code. AI coding agents tend to reproduce common insecure patterns — hard-coded credentials, missing input validation, improper trust boundary enforcement — at scale. The framework recommends that organizations build AI-specific vulnerability profiles that track which patterns their AI coding tools tend to introduce, and prioritize fixes for those patterns even when the individual CVSS scores are moderate.

**The remediation pipeline integration.** The framework also addresses the remediation bottleneck directly, recommending that organizations build automated patch deployment pipelines that can handle the volume of AI-discovered vulnerabilities — and that they establish "fix windows" measured in hours rather than weeks for vulnerabilities in AI infrastructure components.

**Why it matters:** The SecurityWeek framework provides a practical answer to a question that every security team is facing: when AI tools find more vulnerabilities than humans can fix, how do you decide what to fix first? The framework's addition of operational, safety, and business risk dimensions to traditional CVSS scoring provides a triage methodology that can scale with the AI-discovered vulnerability volume. For organizations that have not yet built automated remediation pipelines, the framework makes the case that the bottleneck has shifted from discovery to remediation — and that the organizations that invest in automated patching will be the ones that keep pace.

### CrowdStrike: The Identity Problem Hiding in AI Agent Deployments — Non-Human Identity Management Is the Missing Security Layer

**CrowdStrike has published an analysis of the identity challenges created by AI agent deployments, arguing that the most overlooked security risk in the AI agent revolution is not prompt injection or data leakage — it is the identity problem: AI agents acting on behalf of users with inherited credentials, creating non-human identities that existing identity and access management systems were never designed to handle.** The analysis, published June 24, positions identity management as the critical missing layer in AI agent security architecture. ([CrowdStrike](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/the-identity-problem-hiding-in-ai-agent-deployments/))

**The inherited identity problem.** When an AI agent performs actions on behalf of a user — reading emails, modifying code, accessing databases — it typically does so using the user's credentials. This means the agent inherits all of the user's permissions, including permissions the agent does not need for its specific task. If the agent is compromised or manipulated, the attacker gains the user's full access. This is the classic "confused deputy" problem, applied to AI agents at scale.

**The non-human identity gap.** Most organizations have identity and access management (IAM) systems designed for human users. AI agents are not human users — they operate at machine speed, they can be compromised without the user's knowledge, and they may persist across sessions. CrowdStrike argues that organizations need to create non-human identities (NHIs) for AI agents, with their own credential stores, permission boundaries, and audit trails — separate from the human users who deploy them.

**The MCP identity dimension.** The analysis connects directly to the MCP security challenges that have dominated recent AI security news. When an AI agent connects to tools and data sources through MCP, each connection creates an identity relationship — and if those relationships are not managed with proper authentication and authorization, they become attack surfaces. CrowdStrike's recommendation is that organizations implement agent-specific OAuth flows, short-lived credentials, and behavioral baselines for agent activity.

**Why it matters:** CrowdStrike's analysis identifies a structural gap in AI agent security that most organizations have not yet addressed. The inherited identity problem means that a compromised AI agent is effectively a compromised user account — with all the access that implies. For organizations deploying AI agents in production, the immediate action is to audit what credentials agents are using, whether those credentials are scoped to the minimum necessary permissions, and whether agent activity is logged separately from human activity.

**1. AI model distillation is now an industrial-scale intelligence operation — and the legislative response is already being drafted.** Anthropic's accusation against Alibaba — 28.8 million exchanges, 25,000 fraudulent accounts, targeting the most advanced capabilities — transforms model distillation from a theoretical concern into a documented, large-scale threat. For organizations deploying AI, the implication is that model access controls need to be treated as security controls, not just terms-of-service enforcement — and that the geopolitical dimension of AI security is now inseparable from the technical one.

**2. AI application platforms are accumulating critical vulnerabilities at scale — and tenant isolation is not keeping pace.** DifyTap's cross-tenant data exposure vulnerabilities (CVSS 9.1 and 9.4) affect a platform used by Volvo, Maersk, Panasonic, and Thermo Fisher — and impact more than one million applications. The vulnerabilities allow attackers to wiretap AI conversations, access files across tenants, and exfiltrate prompts and model responses. If your organization runs Dify or any multi-tenant AI platform, the immediate action is to upgrade to the latest version, audit tenant isolation controls, and validate that file-handling mechanisms enforce proper access controls.

**3. CI/CD pipeline security has a structural weakness that affects the entire open-source ecosystem — and AI coding agents are making it worse.** Cordyceps is not a vulnerability in any single product — it is a class of weakness where untrusted PR data crosses trust boundaries without proper permission scoping. It affects Microsoft, Google, Apache, Cloudflare, and the Python Software Foundation simultaneously. Novee's warning that "agentic coding means these vulnerabilities are reproduced persistently, at scale" is a direct challenge to organizations deploying AI coding agents: every AI-generated PR is a potential Cordyceps vector if the CI/CD pipeline is not properly scoped.

**4. The identity problem in AI agent deployments is the most overlooked security risk — and it is about to become urgent.** CrowdStrike's analysis identifies a structural gap: AI agents inherit user credentials without proper scoping, creating non-human identities that existing IAM systems cannot manage. The "confused deputy" problem — where an agent has more permissions than it needs for its specific task — is the root cause of many MCP-based attacks. Organizations deploying AI agents should audit what credentials agents are using, implement agent-specific OAuth flows, and establish behavioral baselines for agent activity.

**5. The AI hardware supply chain is diversifying — and hardware security is becoming an AI security concern.** OpenAI's custom chip built with Broadcom, Qualcomm's acquisition of Modular, and the ongoing GLM-5.2 open agent advancements all point in the same direction: the AI infrastructure stack is becoming more diverse and more vertically integrated. For security teams, the implication is that AI hardware security — hardware-level model protection, supply chain integrity, trusted execution environments — is becoming a relevant evaluation criterion, not just a theoretical concern. The organizations that include hardware security in their AI vendor assessments will be better positioned as the infrastructure landscape shifts.
