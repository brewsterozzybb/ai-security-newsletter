### WAIC 2026: 29 Countries Sign Agreement Creating the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization — Xi Pitches China as Open-Source AI Leader

Shanghai hosted the opening of the **World Artificial Intelligence Conference (WAIC) 2026** on July 17, the same day that **29 countries signed an agreement** to create the **World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization (WAICO)** — the first intergovernmental international body dedicated to AI governance. Chinese President **Xi Jinping** used his keynote to position China as the leader of an "open and cooperative" global AI order, calling on countries to "encourage open source, openness, collaboration and sharing" while warning against any nation "placing its own security interests above those of others."

**A new governance axis.** Xinhua reports that the agreement was signed on July 16, the eve of the conference, after more than a year of preparatory talks. The new organization formalizes what has until now been a series of bilateral and ad-hoc working groups: a standing body with a secretariat, member-state funding, and the authority to convene working groups on specific AI risks. The conference runs through July 20, with the High-Level Meeting on Global AI Governance running in parallel.

**The Chinese counter-frame.** Xi's address was a deliberate reframing of the AI safety debate. He called for a "people-centered" approach to AI and said AI should remain "secure and controllable," language that positions safety as a state-led process rather than a market- or industry-led one. The pitch is aimed squarely at developing nations that have been courted by both the US AI Safety Institute network and the Chinese AI governance framework. CNBC noted that Xi's "security overreach" warning was directed at US export controls on advanced AI chips and the Entity List restrictions on Chinese AI companies.

**The open-source contrast.** The Quartz analysis highlights the gap between Xi's open-source rhetoric and the actual structure of Chinese AI policy. The 360/Qihoo disclosure earlier this week (see the June 30 newsletter) showed that any vulnerability found in China must be reported to MIIT within 48 hours, before the affected vendor is notified. The combination of "open source, open collaboration" on one hand and a state reporting constraint on the other is the kind of inconsistency that will define the next phase of the AI governance debate.

**Why it matters:** For the first time, AI governance has a permanent intergovernmental body with member-state funding and a secretariat. Whether the new organization becomes a venue for genuine coordination or a forum for competing governance visions will shape everything from cross-border AI incident reporting to model registration requirements. The security implication is direct: any multinational organization that operates AI systems across multiple jurisdictions now has a third governance track to monitor alongside national regulators and the EU AI Act. The next 90 days — the duration of WAIC 2026 — will produce the first concrete working-group charters, and those charters will be the first place to look for what the new body will actually do.

### NadMesh Botnet Hunts Exposed AI Services for Cloud Keys and Kubernetes Tokens — MCP Heads the Exploitation Priority List

A Go-based botnet called **NadMesh** has been scanning the internet since early July with a scan queue explicitly tuned for AI services. The operator's own dashboard claims **3,811 unique AWS keys** have been harvested, and the malware's exploitation priority list puts **MCP** ahead of Kubernetes, the Docker API, and Redis. The threat is the first botnet purpose-built around the AI service stack.

**The scan queue.** NadMesh's Shodan harvester keeps its targets stocked with the image generators, local model runners, and workflow builders that teams stand up fast and firewall late: **ComfyUI (port 8188), Ollama (port 11434), n8n (port 5678), Open WebUI, Langflow, and Gradio (port 7860)**. The intel feed behind the operator's credential counter shows **47 credential hauls and 41 model inventories** in its last 100 records. The model inventories carry DeepSeek, GLM, and Kimi identifiers tagged `:cloud`, suggesting the bots catalogue credentials for hosted model services, not just the boxes they scanned.

**What the botnet ships home.** What a NadMesh bot exfiltrates is **cloud keys pulled out of environment variables, Kubernetes service-account tokens, and the contents of `~/.aws/config`, `.env`, and `~/.docker/config.json`**. The researchers put it plainly: the operator is after "not the host itself, but the cloud credentials, Kubernetes cluster privileges" on it. Model access and callable MCP tools round out the list.

**The MCP priority.** The single most consequential detail in the XLab report is that **MCP heads the controller's priority order for exploitation**, above Kubernetes, Docker API, and Redis. The vector XLab records beside it is a `tools/call` JSON-RPC request to `execute_command`. No CVE is attached to that line. The reason matters: **MCP's first specification put authentication outside the core protocol entirely**, and the authorization flow added in March 2025 is still optional. A botnet doesn't need a CVE when the protocol's own design treats the tool server as trustworthy.

**The scale.** Distinct source IPs pushing NadMesh sat near zero through late June, then went vertical in the first week of July to around **139 a day**. XLab's report includes a screenshot of the operator's panel, dated July 10, showing a counter reading 17,700 total deploys above a funnel claiming 95,700 in the past 24 hours. The figures on the panel are the operator's own and they do not agree with each other — but the external sensor data is consistent with a campaign that ramped from nothing in June to a sustained, multi-vector operation in the first half of July.

**The vendor lineage.** The report was published by **QiAnXin's XLab** on July 17. The malware was named after the `n4d mesh controller` string in its source. The reporting structure (Shodan harvester → credential exfil → operator dashboard) is closer to a product-grade threat than a script-kiddie operation. The 20+ RCE vectors XLab documents cover the standard AI service attack surface plus several MCP-server-specific paths.

**Why it matters:** This is the first botnet that exists to attack AI services. The AWS-key counter, the MCP priority, and the explicit targeting of Ollama, ComfyUI, and n8n all point in the same direction: the attacker believes the AI service stack is where the cloud credentials are, and the protocol design (MCP, with auth-out-of-scope) makes the attack cheap. For any organization running an AI service on the public internet, the immediate action is to firewall the AI service ports, rotate any cloud credentials that have ever lived on the same host, and audit the MCP server's authentication posture. The longer-term lesson is that MCP's "auth is out of scope" design choice has now produced its first dedicated threat actor.

### CISA Adds Exploited SharePoint RCE Zero-Day CVE-2026-58644 (CVSS 9.8) to KEV — FCEB Deadline July 19

**CISA** added **CVE-2026-58644**, a critical deserialization-of-untrusted-data vulnerability in Microsoft SharePoint, to the **Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog** on July 16, two days after the patch shipped in Microsoft's July Patch Tuesday release. The vulnerability scores **CVSS 9.8** and allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to execute arbitrary code. CISA set the FCEB remediation deadline at **July 19, 2026** under BOD 26-04.

**The vulnerability.** CVE-2026-58644 is a deserialization flaw that allows an unauthorized attacker to execute arbitrary code on the SharePoint server. The exploit chain leverages the same family of deserialization paths that have been a persistent SharePoint weakness — the kind of vulnerability that has been actively exploited by multiple threat actors across the 2026 calendar year. CISA's updated alert (originally posted July 14) now lists four actively-exploited SharePoint CVEs in 2026 alone: **CVE-2026-32201, CVE-2026-45659, CVE-2026-56164, and CVE-2026-58644**.

**The other exploited SharePoint CVE.** CISA also added **CVE-2026-56164** (SharePoint Server elevation of privilege, remotely exploitable without authentication, CVSS 5.3) to KEV on July 14 — the same Patch Tuesday release. SecurityWeek reports that the vulnerability was exploited "soon after disclosure," the same operational pattern that made LegacyHive (covered in the July 16 newsletter) a working exploit within hours of Patch Tuesday.

**The detection problem.** SharePoint deserialization exploits typically leave little forensic trace. The attack vector is the standard SharePoint HTTP request path, the payload is processed in-process, and the resulting code execution inherits the SharePoint application pool's identity. For organizations that have not yet applied the July 14 patch, the time between disclosure (July 14) and the FCEB deadline (July 19) is five days, but the time between active exploitation in the wild and patch application is what matters. CISA's two-day gap between KEV addition (July 16) and the FCEB deadline (July 19) is the standard BOD 26-04 three-day cycle.

**Why it matters:** A CVSS 9.8 unauthenticated RCE in a product as widely deployed as SharePoint, with confirmed active exploitation in the wild, is the standard high-priority KEV addition. The AI security connection is less direct than the connection to enterprise file and content management: SharePoint hosts the document stores, list data, and metadata that AI agents and copilots read and write. An attacker with SharePoint code execution can pivot to the AI agents that read from those stores. For organizations running Microsoft 365 Copilot, SharePoint Online Agents, or any third-party AI service that integrates with SharePoint, the patch is critical: a compromised SharePoint server is a compromised data source for every AI agent that reads it.

## 3. Technical Deep Dives

### Prismata: UC Berkeley System-Level Defense Against Cross-Site Prompting — Biba Integrity Model Slashes Attack Success from 85.5% to 0.7%

A group at **UC Berkeley** has published a system-level defense against the agent-era equivalent of **Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)**: **Cross-Site Prompting (XSP)**. Their system, **Prismata**, sits between a web agent and the browser, filtering the content an agent sees and constraining the actions the agent can take. In testing against the **WebArena** benchmark (a real GitLab instance plus a production-grade shopping platform), the system reduced XSP attack success from **85.5% to 0.7%** — a 122x reduction.

**The XSP parallel.** XSS lets an attacker plant a script on a trusted site so it runs in a visitor's browser. **XSP swaps executable code for plain language**: an attacker writes a product review that tells the agent to send the user's credit card details to a stranger, and the agent may comply. Older web defenses translate poorly to this setting: input sanitizers screen for code, and prompt injections carry none. The Berkeley team is explicit that prompt injection is the "new XSS threat in the AI agent era."

**The page-tree insight.** The key empirical observation is that for any button, link, or form field on a page, there is a path of ancestor elements running from the top of the page down to that element. Across more than **90,000 samples of untrusted content** drawn from Common Crawl and Mind2Web, only about **1.2%** sat on a path leading to something the agent could click or fill. The rest were dead ends for an attacker. This means that the attack surface for XSP is not the page text — it is the path of structural ancestors of actionable elements.

**The Biba model.** For the 1.2% of cases where untrusted content does sit on an actionable path, Prismata borrows from a **1977 security design**. The **Biba integrity model** enforces a no-read-down, no-write-up rule, and the Berkeley team applies it to the page tree. The system reads each layer of the path before revealing its children, watching for signals that untrusted content is coming: headings like "Reviews," accessibility labels, and developer class names like `review-card` mark the boundary. When a signal appears, everything below it inherits a lower trust label and gets pruned or set to read-only.

**The reduction mechanism.** A reasoning model is asked to read only the path and the user's task, then decide whether the element belongs to the task. An injection sitting elsewhere on the page stays out of that decision. The same reasoning model that an attacker might target with a planted instruction is now being used to make a structural decision before the instruction is ever seen.

**The evaluation.** The tests ran in WebArena, which is about as close to the live web as a benchmark gets. It runs a real GitLab instance and a production-grade shopping platform, so the page structures resemble what an agent would meet in the wild. The attacks were pop-up-style lures planted where a malicious user or ad network could plausibly put them. The 85.5% baseline attack success rate is what the same agent achieves without Prismata; the 0.7% is with the system in place.

**Why it matters:** Prismata is the first published system-level defense against XSP that operates at the structural layer rather than the content layer. The key insight is that the attack surface is the path, not the page text — and the path is overwhelmingly developer-written scaffolding. The Biba model gives a 47-year-old security primitive a new application. For organizations deploying web agents in production, the practical lesson is that page-tree filtering can be implemented as a middleware layer between the browser and the agent, without modifying the agent itself. The defense is deployable today as a guardrail for any web agent that reads user-supplied content, including product reviews, comments, and user-generated listings.

### DeepMind's Hassabis Calls for US-Led Global AI Watchdog with Power to Pause Development — "Warning Shots" Today, Nuclear Threats in 18 Months

**Demis Hassabis**, CEO of Google DeepMind, used an exclusive interview with **Axios** to call for a **US-led global AI watchdog** with the authority to pause development, to be operational by year-end 2026. Hassabis framed today's AI-driven cyber risks as "warning shots" and projected that **within 18 months, more serious biological and nuclear risks could live inside open-source models beyond any government's control**. The intervention comes one day before WAIC 2026 opens in Shanghai and three days before the first meeting of the new World AI Cooperation Organization (see Headliners).

**The proposed structure.** Hassabis's "security gate" is modeled on the **International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)**: a standing body with technical expertise, member-state funding, and the authority to inspect, audit, and pause AI development above a defined capability threshold. The framing is explicit — the alternative, Hassabis argued, is the open-source release of models with capabilities for biological or nuclear harm before any government can act. The Cryptopolitan coverage notes that the timeline is critical: "18 months" places the proposed watchdog as a defensive measure against a near-term threat, not a hypothetical one.

**The cyber framing.** Hassabis's "warning shots" framing covers the agentic-attacker scenario (the HuggingFace breach from the July 17 newsletter), the AI-augmented vulnerability discovery that produced Microsoft's record-breaking July Patch Tuesday (covered in the July 16 newsletter), and the botnet-style attacks on AI service endpoints (the NadMesh story above). The argument is that today's AI-driven attacks are reconnaissance, not exploitation at scale.

**The US positioning.** Hassabis was explicit that the watchdog must be **US-led**. The framing puts the US in the role of the convener of global AI governance — a position the US AI Safety Institute network has been building toward since 2024. The proposed watchdog is, in effect, a more authoritative version of the AI Safety Institute: same model, more teeth. The argument is that without US leadership, the World AI Cooperation Organization (created at WAIC 2026) will become the de facto venue for global AI governance — and that venue will be shaped by China's policy preferences.

**The open-source tension.** The most important nuance is the open-source framing. Hassabis's warning is that open-source models are the channel through which dangerous capabilities could escape any single government's control. The implication is that capability-based export controls and model release decisions need international coordination — and that coordination, in his framing, must be US-led. The framing aligns Hassabis with the US export-control establishment and against the more permissive open-source release position that has prevailed at Meta and Mistral.

**Why it matters:** Hassabis is the most senior AI lab leader to call for a global AI watchdog with the authority to pause development. The intervention lands in the same week that the EU AI Act compliance deadline approaches and that the first intergovernmental AI organization is created. For enterprise security teams, the practical implication is that AI governance is about to become a multi-tracked international regulatory activity. Audit your AI inventory against all three tracks — the US AI Safety Institute, the EU AI Act, and the new WAICO — because the next 90 days will produce the first concrete compliance frameworks from each.

## 6. Five Things to Take Into the Rest of the Day

1. **Treat every AI service endpoint you expose as a credential-magnet, not a feature flag.** The NadMesh botnet exists because the AI service stack — ComfyUI, Ollama, n8n, Open WebUI, Langflow, Gradio — is the place where the cloud credentials are. The immediate action: firewall every AI service port, rotate any cloud credentials that have ever lived on the same host, and audit the MCP server's authentication posture. The longer-term lesson is that MCP's "authentication is out of scope" design choice has now produced its first dedicated threat actor. Any MCP server you deploy needs to be on a private network, behind a VPN, or fronted by an authenticating gateway — not on the public internet with a "we'll add auth later" plan.

2. **Patch SharePoint for CVE-2026-58644 (CVSS 9.8) by July 19, even if your fleet is mixed Dell/Windows 11.** CISA's KEV addition is the standard three-day FCEB cycle, and the actively-exploited status means attackers are already scanning. For organizations with the Microsoft July 14 patch applied and a current SharePoint build, the action is to verify the patch is in place and to monitor for the deserialization indicators Microsoft published. For organizations with the KB5101650 update blocked on Dell devices (see Daily Feed), the SharePoint patch is server-side and is not affected by the client-side Intel driver conflict — patch the SharePoint server separately and don't let the client-side update issue delay the server-side fix.

3. **Run Prismata-style page-tree filtering for any web agent that reads user-supplied content.** UC Berkeley's Prismata shows that the attack surface for XSP is the path of structural ancestors of actionable elements — and that path is overwhelmingly developer-written scaffolding. The practical implementation is a middleware layer between the browser and the agent that reads the page tree, marks user-content boundaries, and prunes or read-onlys everything below. The defense is deployable today as a guardrail for any web agent that reads product reviews, comments, or user-generated listings. The 122x reduction (85.5% to 0.7%) is the most concrete number we have for an XSP defense that doesn't require retraining the model.

4. **Map the WAIC 2026 commitments to your compliance roadmap, and add the new WAICO to your regulatory watchlist.** The 29-country agreement creating the **World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO)** is the first permanent intergovernmental AI governance body. The next 90 days — the duration of WAIC 2026 — will produce the first concrete working-group charters, and those charters will define what the new body actually does. For multinational organizations, the practical action is to add WAICO to the regulatory watchlist alongside the EU AI Act (compliance deadline August 2026) and the US AI Safety Institute network.
