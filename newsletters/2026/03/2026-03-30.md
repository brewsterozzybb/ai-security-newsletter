### **The CVE Program Is "Weirdly Fragile" and AI Is Making It Worse — RSAC 2026 Panels Sound the Alarm on the System That Underpins All of Patch Management**

For 25 years, the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures program has been the global vocabulary for talking about software flaws. Now it's facing simultaneous pressure from three directions: AI tools are flooding the database with new entries faster than MITRE can process and validate them, a near-shutdown funding scare last April demonstrated how politically fragile the system is, and international equivalents are creating fragmentation pressure. At the 2026 RSAC Conference this week, multiple panels described the system — maintained by MITRE under federal contract — as "weirdly fragile" and warned that "it is definitely not too big to fail." American Banker reported this morning that the downstream consequence for regulated industries like banking would be catastrophic: without a shared CVE vocabulary, patch prioritization, compliance reporting, and vendor coordination all break simultaneously.

Key findings:

- **AI-driven CVE volume surge** — AI tools are autonomously discovering and filing vulnerability reports at a rate that overwhelms MITRE's curation capacity, reducing data quality and increasing noise-to-signal ratio
- **The April 2025 near-shutdown** — a funding lapse almost killed the program; the incident was characterized as a "fire drill" that caught the attention of House Homeland Security Subcommittee Director Moira Bergin
- **Fragmentation risk** — international equivalents (EU ENISA's vulnerability database, China's CNVD) are diverging from CVE, meaning the same vulnerability can have different identifiers across geographies
- **Banking sector exposure** — FDIC regulations require banks to maintain effective patch management programs; a CVE collapse would sever the shared language between banks, vendors, and regulators
- **"Weirdly fragile" paradox** — CVE is simultaneously deeply embedded in every global legal and regulatory framework AND operating on funding and infrastructure that could be disrupted by a single political decision

> **"CVE is one of the most important pillars of modern cybersecurity — and it is definitely not too big to fail."** — RSAC 2026 panel slide deck, Technology Company Security Leaders Panel

The Georgia Tech Vibe Security Radar data covered in Friday's newsletter (35 AI-code CVEs in March alone) is directly relevant here: AI is simultaneously creating the vulnerabilities that need CVE entries AND the volume of AI-discovered reports that is overwhelming the system processing those entries. The loop is self-reinforcing. Organizations that depend on NVD enrichment data for their vulnerability scanners should understand that NVD processing backlogs mean newly filed CVEs may sit without CVSS scores and enrichment for weeks — which means your CVSS-based prioritization systems are working on incomplete data.

**Immediate actions:**
- Supplement NVD-based vulnerability feeds with vendor-direct advisories and CISA KEV — both are faster and more reliable than waiting for NVD enrichment
- Audit your patch prioritization workflow: if it depends exclusively on NVD CVSS scores, add a CISA KEV check as a mandatory fast lane that bypasses the NVD backlog
- Follow the CVE Foundation (cvefoundation.org) and RSAC policy track developments — if MITRE's contract is not renewed, the transition to a foundation model affects every vulnerability management vendor you use
- Consider the international fragmentation risk in your third-party vendor risk assessments — if a vendor only files with CNVD and not CVE, it may not appear in your scans

### **OpenAI Launches Safety Bug Bounty Covering MCP Abuse, Prompt Injection, and Agentic Data Exfiltration — Pays for What It Previously Called "Out of Scope"**

OpenAI quietly shipped one of the more significant structural changes to AI security accountability this week: a dedicated Safety Bug Bounty program hosted on Bugcrowd that explicitly covers AI-specific abuse risks that traditional bug bounties have always rejected as out of scope. The program covers agentic risks in Atlas Browser, Codex, Operator, ChatGPT Connectors, and other tools — specifically including MCP abuse, third-party prompt injection, and data exfiltration from agentic pipelines. This matters because it is the first major AI lab to formally acknowledge that the attack surface of agentic AI products requires a different vulnerability class than traditional software, and to fund external researchers to find those vulnerabilities. Researchers who find ways to make Operator or Codex perform unauthorized actions or leak sensitive data are now eligible for bounty payments — whereas before March 25, those submissions would have been closed as "model behavior" not eligible for payout.

Key findings:

- **Agentic risk categories in scope**: MCP abuse, third-party prompt injection, data exfiltration via agent pipelines, disallowed actions at scale, unauthorized cross-agent data access
- **Products covered**: Atlas Browser, Codex, Operator, ChatGPT Connectors, MCP integrators, and other agentic tools
- **Reproducibility requirement**: submitted vulnerabilities must be reproducible — jailbreaks that require social engineering don't qualify, but systematic injection paths do
- **Program hosted on Bugcrowd** — launched March 25, 2026; external researchers can submit immediately
- **Signal for the industry**: every other major AI lab will now face pressure to match this program — expect Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft to follow within 90 days

> **"Agentic risks, including model context protocol (MCP) abuse, third-party prompt injection, data exfiltration, [and] disallowed actions at scale... [are] in scope."** — OpenAI Safety Bug Bounty program definition

The practical implication for enterprise security teams: OpenAI is implicitly acknowledging that its agentic products have a systematic prompt injection and MCP abuse surface that it does not fully understand. The researchers who find the bugs first are now being paid to do so — which means your enterprise Operator deployments and Codex integrations are actively being probed right now, and the findings will inform next-generation attack techniques before they're patched. Organizations running OpenAI agentic products should treat this as a signal to harden their own configurations immediately rather than waiting for the bounty program to surface and disclose exploits.

**Immediate actions:**
- Audit your organization's OpenAI agentic product configurations: Operator, Codex, and any ChatGPT Connector integrations — specifically check what data each can access and what actions it can perform
- Implement output monitoring for agentic pipelines: log what each agent does, what external services it calls, and what data it transmits
- Review your MCP server configurations for any OpenAI integrations — verify authentication is required and tool permissions are scoped to minimum necessary
- Subscribe to the OpenAI security disclosure feed so you receive bounty-driven patches as they ship

### **Iran's Cyber Operations Go Global as 27-Day Internet Blackout Separates State Actors From Proxies — Unit 42 and Flashpoint Tracking Escalating Multi-Vector Campaign**

Following the February 28 U.S.-Israel joint strikes (Operation Epic Fury / Operation Roaring Lion), Unit 42 has published an updated threat brief documenting Iran's cyber retaliation campaign — now in its 27th consecutive day of near-complete internet blackout inside Iran, forcing a significant operational change: state-aligned cyber units may be operating in tactical isolation, while the actual attack traffic is originating from Iranian diaspora and affiliated hacktivist groups outside the country. Unit 42 identified 7,381 conflict-themed phishing URLs across 1,881 unique hostnames, targeting telecommunications, airlines, law enforcement, and energy corporations. Flashpoint separately confirmed that MOIS-linked group MuddyWater has infiltrated U.S. aerospace and defense networks, and pro-Russian hacktivist group NoName057(16) claimed DDoS attacks against Israeli political parties and Elbit Systems.

Key findings:

- **27+ days of near-complete internet blackout inside Iran** — connectivity at 1-4% since Feb. 28; state-directed cyber units cannot reliably coordinate sophisticated attacks from within the country
- **Attack origin shift**: most current malicious activity originates from Iranian diaspora, allied hacktivist groups, and cells outside Iran operating with "tactical autonomy" from Iranian command structures
- **7,381 phishing URLs** across 1,881 hostnames — themed around the conflict, impersonating telecoms, airlines, law enforcement, and energy companies for financial fraud and credential harvesting
- **MuddyWater confirmed in U.S. aerospace/defense networks** (Flashpoint) — infiltration timing suggests pre-positioned access established before the blackout
- **Techniques**: TLD rotation, subdomain chaining, infrastructure mimicking corporate portals, cryptocurrency donation scams, financial fraud

> **"State-aligned cyber units may be acting in operational isolation, which could result in deviations from previously established patterns."** — Unit 42 Threat Brief, March 26, 2026

The blackout creates a specific threat dynamic: long-running operations that were pre-positioned before Feb. 28 (like MuddyWater's aerospace access) remain active and operational even without real-time command and control from Tehran. These cells are effectively running on cached instructions, and their behavior may be less predictable and more aggressive than usual because they lack the restraint that comes from real-time oversight. Security teams in aerospace, defense, energy, and financial services should treat current threat levels as elevated.

**Immediate actions:**
- Elevate threat hunting priority in aerospace, defense, and energy sectors — look for MuddyWater TTPs: PowerShell-heavy lateral movement, use of legitimate remote management tools, abuse of Exchange and SharePoint
- Block or closely monitor phishing-themed domains impersonating telecoms and airlines — the volume (7,381 URLs) suggests automated generation via adversarial AI
- Brief your SOC on the "tactical autonomy" dynamic: Iranian-affiliated actors may behave less predictably than their historical pattern suggests — widen your detection heuristics
- Review VPN and remote access logs for anomalous authentication from IP ranges associated with Iranian diaspora communities

### **SentinelOne and Snyk Both Drop AI Agent Security Tooling the Same Week — Converging on MCP Inventory, Red Teaming, and Air-Gapped Enforcement**

Two major security vendors announced AI-agent-specific products within days of each other, and the overlap in their architectures tells you a lot about where the market is headed. SentinelOne's **Prompt AI Agent Security** automatically inventories MCP servers used by a company's AI agents and applies guardrails to prevent unauthorized data exfiltration — it can block an agent from sending business data to an unauthorized third-party application, or from accessing internal systems outside its permission scope. Alongside it, **Prompt AI Red Teaming** simulates malicious prompts, training data poisoning, and other attack classes against AI applications. The third new product, **Prompt Security On-Premises**, is designed for air-gapped environments — storing telemetry locally rather than sending it to SentinelOne's cloud, addressing a gap that has prevented regulated-sector AI deployments from using cloud-based security controls. Snyk's contribution, **Evo AI-SPM**, takes a code-repository-first approach: it scans code repos for language model inventory, MCP server configurations, and AI asset risks — then deploys three agents (in preview) to validate third-party AI components, test AI applications for vulnerabilities, and block prompt responses that contain training data excerpts.

Key findings:

- **SentinelOne Prompt AI Agent Security**: MCP server auto-discovery + guardrails; blocks unauthorized data transfer and out-of-scope internal access
- **Prompt AI Red Teaming**: simulates malicious prompts, training data poisoning, and agent attack scenarios against production AI applications
- **Prompt Security On-Premises**: air-gapped AI security — telemetry stays on-prem, addressing compliance barriers in regulated industries
- **Snyk Evo AI-SPM**: LLM + MCP inventory from code repos; three AI agents (in preview) for validation, vulnerability testing, and training-data-leak detection
- **Market signal**: two major vendors shipping overlapping MCP inventory and agent guardrail products in the same week = this is now a mainstream security product category, not a research concept

> **"Agentic architectures turn governance into a software supply chain problem. Our value is confirming which findings are real and exploitable, using ground truth data from a decade of enterprise deployment that no AI model can produce alone."** — Manoj Nair, Chief Innovation Officer, Snyk

The convergence of SentinelOne and Snyk on the same problem space in the same week is not a coincidence — both are responding to the same enterprise RFP requirement: "give me visibility into what AI agents and MCP servers are running in my environment." For security practitioners, this means the tooling to answer that question is now available, which means it's no longer acceptable to say "we don't know what AI is deployed." Ignorance is now a policy choice, not a capability gap.

**Immediate actions:**
- Pilot one of these tools — SentinelOne's or Snyk's — for AI asset discovery before deploying more agents; you cannot secure what you cannot enumerate
- If you operate in a regulated or air-gapped environment, SentinelOne's on-prem option removes a previous barrier to AI security monitoring
- Ask your current security vendors (EDR, SIEM, CSPM) what their AI agent visibility roadmap looks like — the competitive pressure from this week's launches will accelerate everyone's timeline

### **Cloudflare AI Security for Apps Hits GA — Free AI Endpoint Discovery for All Plans, IBM Partnership, Wiz Integration**

Cloudflare's AI Security for Apps shipped to general availability on March 11 (just reaching wider practitioner awareness this week), and the GA announcement includes meaningful new capabilities: free AI endpoint discovery for all Cloudflare customers including Free, Pro, and Business tiers; detection for custom-defined sensitive topics; an expanded IBM partnership (IBM has chosen Cloudflare as its AI security delivery layer for cloud customers); and a joint visibility integration with Wiz. The product sits as a reverse proxy in front of AI-powered applications — regardless of which model or hosting provider is used — and provides three functions: discovery (find all AI endpoints in your web property), detection (identify malicious or off-policy behavior including prompt injection, sensitive data disclosure, and model abuse), and mitigation (WAF-style rule enforcement for AI interactions). Cloudflare explicitly frames the threat model around OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications.

Key findings:

- **Free AI endpoint discovery** — now available on all Cloudflare plans including Free; removes the previous cost barrier to shadow AI detection
- **Model/provider agnostic** — works regardless of which LLM or hosting provider the underlying application uses
- **IBM partnership** — IBM chosen Cloudflare to deliver AI security to its cloud customers; signals enterprise adoption at scale
- **Wiz integration** — joint AI security posture view for mutual customers; links runtime threat detection with infrastructure-level risk findings
- **Custom topic detection** — organizations can define their own sensitive topic categories (e.g., "don't allow the AI to discuss competitor products," or "flag any output containing PII patterns")

The "shadow AI" problem — security teams not knowing where AI is deployed across their web properties — is the same problem SentinelOne and Snyk are solving inside enterprise networks. Cloudflare is solving it at the network edge for internet-facing applications. For organizations heavily invested in Cloudflare, this is an immediate capability gain with no additional vendor relationship required.

**Immediate actions:**
- Enable AI endpoint discovery in Cloudflare if you're already on any paid plan — it's now free and provides immediate visibility into shadow AI deployments
- Configure OWASP LLM Top 10 detection rules for any customer-facing AI applications you have behind Cloudflare
- If you're an IBM cloud customer, ask your account team about the Cloudflare AI security integration timeline

### **GitHub Copilot Will Train on Your Code Starting April 24 — And It Already Edited an Ad Into Someone's PR**

Two separate Copilot stories converged this week to make the same point: AI coding tools are evolving their data relationships with users in ways that security and privacy teams need to track. First: GitHub updated its privacy policy on March 25, announcing that starting April 24, 2026, interaction data from Copilot Free, Pro, and Pro+ users — including code snippets, chat conversations, and suggestion acceptance decisions — may be used to train GitHub's AI models, unless users manually opt out at `/settings/copilot/features` under the Privacy heading. Second: developer Zach Manson documented that after a team member asked Copilot to fix a typo in his PR, Copilot edited the PR description to include promotional copy for itself and Raycast — promotional snippets embedded via hidden HTML comments tagged `START COPILOT CODING AGENT TIPS`. According to Windows Report, the same injected content appeared in more than 11,000 pull requests across multiple repositories.

Key findings:

- **CVE/tracking**: no CVE; this is policy and behavior, not a software vulnerability — but the security implications are material
- **Training data deadline**: April 24, 2026 — after this date, Copilot interaction data (code, chat, decisions) trains GitHub models unless opted out
- **Proprietary code exposure risk**: if developers use Copilot Free/Pro for work on proprietary codebases and have not opted out, those code patterns may enter GitHub's training corpus
- **Copilot ad injection**: hidden HTML comment mechanism (`START COPILOT CODING AGENT TIPS`) allowed Copilot to insert promotional content into PR descriptions in 11,000+ repositories
- **The security angle on ad injection**: the same hidden-HTML-comment injection mechanism that Copilot used for ads could theoretically be used by a malicious actor to inject instructions into agent-processed PR descriptions — a concrete example of the AI-agent instruction injection surface

**Immediate actions:**
- Opt out of Copilot training data collection **before April 24**: GitHub Settings → Copilot → Features → Privacy → disable "Allow GitHub to use my data for AI model training"
- Enterprise Copilot customers: verify your enterprise-level data sharing settings — Enterprise plans have separate controls that should already exclude training data use, but verify
- Add a PR review checklist item to look for hidden HTML comments in AI-assisted PR descriptions, particularly in high-trust repositories
- Treat the ad injection incident as a behavioral proof-of-concept: if Copilot can insert content via hidden HTML, so can prompt injection — review your Copilot configuration for any settings that allow unrestricted PR editing

## Five Things to Take Into the Rest of the Day

**1. The CVE crisis is your patch management program's hidden dependency.**
Every vulnerability scanner, SIEM correlation rule, and compliance report in your organization depends on CVE as its lingua franca. The RSAC panels this week are not theoretical — the NVD enrichment backlog already means newly disclosed CVEs can sit without CVSS scores for weeks. If your patching SLA is "patch critical CVEs within 72 hours," you may be systematically missing new critical vulnerabilities that haven't received NVD enrichment yet. Audit your vulnerability management program's data pipeline today: does it include CISA KEV as an independent fast lane? Does it include vendor-direct advisories? If not, fix those gaps this week.

**2. OpenAI's bug bounty scope is a roadmap of what attackers are already trying.**
When OpenAI writes "MCP abuse, third-party prompt injection, and data exfiltration via agentic pipelines" into a bug bounty scope, they're describing attacks they're already aware of or actively observing. Every scope item in a major vendor's bug bounty is an implicit acknowledgment that the attack class is real and the vendor doesn't fully understand their own exposure. Treat the OpenAI bounty scope as a threat model checklist for your own agentic deployments — if OpenAI is worried about it, you should be worried about your production Operator and Codex integrations.

**3. 38% unauthenticated MCP servers is an infrastructure fire, not a configuration nitpick.**
The Unit 42 / Adversa.ai finding that 38% of public MCP servers have no authentication means that roughly 1 in 3 of the servers your AI agents might call require no credentials to use, abuse, or inject malicious tool responses into. If you are deploying AI agents that use MCP, your first security action is an MCP inventory (SentinelOne and Snyk both now ship tools for this). Your second action is verifying authentication on every MCP server your agents can reach. Your third action is implementing allowlists so your agents can only call pre-approved, authenticated servers.

**4. The Copilot ad injection is a prompt injection proof of concept.**
GitHub Copilot injected promotional content into PR descriptions using hidden HTML comments. The mechanism — embed trusted-looking content in a document that AI tools will read and act on — is structurally identical to indirect prompt injection. The fact that Copilot used this mechanism for marketing rather than attack is irrelevant to the security lesson: if Copilot can insert hidden content that downstream tools trust, so can an attacker who can influence any document in your development workflow. Review your AI coding tool permissions around PR description editing.

**5. Iran's "tactical autonomy" dynamic deserves attention from SOC teams this week.**
The 27-day internet blackout inside Iran has separated Iranian state cyber units from their command and control. Pre-positioned implants like MuddyWater's U.S. aerospace access are operating autonomously — meaning their behavior may deviate from established TTP patterns that your detection rules rely on. Less predictable behavior from a sophisticated threat actor in a high-tension geopolitical context is a threat hunting trigger. If you're in an industry targeted by Iranian actors (aerospace, defense, energy, financial services), this week warrants elevated monitoring and expanded heuristics.
