### **Cloudflare's 2026 Threat Report: DDoS Doubled, Record 31.4 Tbps Strike, and the Rise of "Living Off the XaaS"**

Cloudflare's Cloudforce One team released the inaugural 2026 Threat Report today, representing a year of analysis across trillions of network signals. The headline finding is a fundamental shift in attacker psychology: threat actors have abandoned the pursuit of technical sophistication in favor of **Measure of Effectiveness (MOE)** — a cold calculation of effort-to-outcome ratio that is reshaping every stage of the kill chain. Why burn a zero-day when a stolen session token bypasses MFA entirely? Why build C2 infrastructure when Google Calendar, Dropbox, and GitHub blend seamlessly into enterprise traffic?

Key findings from the report:

- **DDoS volume doubled in 2025**, with hyper-volumetric attacks up 700% at the network layer; the Aisuru botnet drove a record 31.4 Tbps strike
- **Session token theft** via LummaC2 and similar infostealers is now the dominant MFA bypass technique — authenticating as the user, not breaking the authentication
- **Salt Typhoon and Linen Typhoon** (China-linked) are systematically pre-positioning in North American telecom, government, and commercial IT for long-term geopolitical leverage
- **North Korea** has operationalized deepfake-enabled remote IT worker infiltration at scale — state operatives embedded in Western payrolls via synthetic identities
- **AI is enabling low-skill actors** to conduct high-impact operations: real-time network mapping, exploit development, and deepfake creation are no longer specialist capabilities
- **SaaS as attack infrastructure**: Amazon SES, SendGrid, and legitimate cloud APIs are increasingly weaponized to camouflage C2 traffic inside benign enterprise signals

> **"In 2026, the most dangerous threat actors aren't the ones with the most advanced code; it's the ones who can integrate intelligence and technology into a single, continuous system that achieves their mission in the shortest time possible."** — Cloudforce One, 2026 Threat Report

The report's framing of the "Living off the XaaS" concept — where attackers wear the uniform of trusted cloud providers — is the conceptual evolution of LOTL (Living off the Land) adapted for cloud-native environments. Every organization running security monitoring that isn't explicitly tuned for SaaS-originated C2 traffic is running blind to one of 2026's primary attacker techniques.

**Immediate actions:**
- Audit your SIEM/SOAR rules for detection coverage against cloud SaaS platforms as C2 sources (Google Drive, Dropbox, GitHub webhooks)
- Implement session token monitoring and anomaly detection — MFA bypass via token theft is already the dominant technique
- Review your organization's exposure to North Korean remote IT worker fraud: verify all remote contractor identities with video + out-of-band verification before onboarding
- Add Cloudforce One's threat intelligence feed to your threat intel pipeline; the 2026 report includes specific IOCs for tracked campaigns

### **CVE-2026-3055 (CVSS 9.3): Citrix NetScaler ADC Under Active Recon — Attackers Fingerprinting SAML IDP Configs Before Mass Exploitation**

A critical memory overread vulnerability in Citrix NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway — disclosed March 23, 2026 — has entered active reconnaissance phase, with multiple threat intelligence honeypot operators confirming attacker probing within days of disclosure. CVE-2026-3055 (CVSS 9.3) stems from insufficient input validation in appliances configured as a **SAML Identity Provider (SAML IDP)**, allowing unauthenticated attackers to trigger an out-of-bounds memory read that can leak active session tokens directly from device memory. Help Net Security confirmed the capability: CVE-2026-3055 can extract live session tokens, making it effectively a SAML-authenticated session hijacking primitive.

Defused Cyber and watchTowr both reported honeypot detections:

- **Defused Cyber**: "We are now observing auth method fingerprinting activity against NetScaler ADC/Gateway in the wild. Attackers are probing `/cgi/GetAuthMethods` to enumerate enabled authentication flows."
- **watchTowr**: "When attacker reconnaissance shifts to active exploitation, the window to respond will evaporate."

| Affected Version | Vulnerable | Patched |
|---|---|---|
| NetScaler ADC 14.1 | < 14.1-66.59 | ≥ 14.1-66.59 |
| NetScaler Gateway 13.1 | < 13.1-62.23 | ≥ 13.1-62.23 |
| NetScaler ADC 13.1-FIPS | < 13.1-37.262 | ≥ 13.1-37.262 |
| NetScaler ADC 13.1-NDcPP | < 13.1-37.262 | ≥ 13.1-37.262 |

NetScaler has been a recurring exploitation target — CVE-2023-4966 (Citrix Bleed), CVE-2025-5777 (Citrix Bleed 2), CVE-2025-6543, and CVE-2025-7775 all saw rapid weaponization after disclosure. The SAML IDP configuration required for exploitation is common in enterprise SSO architectures. The question is not if CVE-2026-3055 gets weaponized — it's when, and NCSC UK issued an advisory alongside Citrix's bulletin.

**Immediate actions:**
- Patch NetScaler ADC/Gateway to patched versions **this weekend** — do not wait for your regular patch cycle
- If your NetScaler is configured as a SAML IDP (check: `show authentication samlIdPProfile`), treat this as critical priority
- Monitor for `/cgi/GetAuthMethods` probe requests in your ADC access logs — active probing is already underway
- If you cannot patch immediately, restrict management access and consider temporarily disabling SAML IDP functionality until patched

### **Anthropic Left "Claude Mythos" Sitting on an Open S3 Bucket — 3,000 Unpublished Assets Exposed, Including Details of a Model the Company Says Poses "Unprecedented Cybersecurity Risks"**

In what Anthropic acknowledged as a security breach, approximately 3,000 unpublished assets — including documentation for an unreleased model codenamed **"Claude Mythos"** — were left in an unsecured data store accessible to anyone with the URL. Fortune's Bea Nolan discovered and reported the exposure. The leaked materials describe Mythos as a "step change" in capability from current Claude models — and, critically, Anthropic's own documentation characterizes Mythos as posing **"unprecedented cybersecurity risks"**, placing it in the same category as frontier models that have crossed a new capability threshold for offensive use.

The exposure included:

- **Claude Mythos technical documentation** — capability assessments, internal benchmarks, architecture notes (not confirmed: model weights)
- **Exclusive CEO event materials** — briefing documents not intended for public release
- **PDFs and images** across approximately 3,000 unpublished assets
- **Anthropic's internal safety framing** for Mythos — language indicating the company believes the model's capabilities warrant heightened pre-deployment scrutiny

> **Anthropic confirmed it is testing a model that "crosses a threshold that the companies say poses new cybersecurity risks."** — Fortune, March 27, 2026

This incident is notable for two compounding reasons. First, the content of the leak: a major AI lab's assessment that its own forthcoming model poses unprecedented offensive security risk is significant irrespective of how it was disclosed. Second, the mechanism: an unsecured cloud data store exposing thousands of internal assets is a textbook cloud misconfiguration failure — the same attack class that's compromised enterprise data across hundreds of organizations. Anthropic, of all companies, should have secrets management and data store ACLs as a baseline security control.

**Immediate actions:**
- Use this as a forcing function for a cloud storage ACL audit — enumerate every S3 bucket, GCS bucket, or Azure Blob container in your org and verify public access is disabled at the bucket policy level, not just the object level
- For AI labs specifically: model documentation, capability assessments, and pre-release technical materials should be classified at least equivalent to internal corporate IP and stored accordingly
- If your org has a data store containing pre-release product documentation, check that it cannot be accessed without authentication — "unpublished" is not the same as "private"

### **TeamPCP Campaign: The "Trivy → Checkmarx → LiteLLM" Supply Chain Chain Reaction (CVE-2026-33634, CVSS 9.4)**

The TeamPCP threat actor's supply chain campaign — first detected via the Trivy GitHub Actions compromise covered in prior editions — has been fully documented by Arctic Wolf and Hackaday as a three-stage chain reaction that ultimately targeted AI provider API keys across enterprise Python environments. The campaign's final stage was the poisoning of **LiteLLM versions 1.82.7 and 1.82.8** on PyPI: malicious code injected via a `.pth` file that auto-executes on Python startup, silently harvesting AI provider API keys (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Mistral), Kubernetes secrets, and cloud credentials from any system where LiteLLM was installed.

The attack chain:

| Stage | Target | Method | Payload |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trivy security scanner | CI/CD secret theft via compromised runner | Establishes attacker in CI/CD pipeline |
| 2 | Checkmarx KICS GitHub Action | Stolen secrets from Stage 1 | Malicious KICS action that harvests further secrets |
| 3 | LiteLLM PyPI package (CVE-2026-33634, CVSS 9.4) | Poisoned package via compromised maintainer creds | `.pth` auto-execution harvests all AI API keys + cloud creds |

The LiteLLM compromise is particularly damaging because LiteLLM is an AI model gateway used by organizations to proxy requests across multiple AI providers — a single installation typically has API keys for every major AI provider configured. Over 1,000 enterprise SaaS environments are estimated to have been impacted.

> **"Security scanners themselves are being weaponized to extract the very secrets they are meant to protect."** — Arctic Wolf, TeamPCP Campaign Analysis

**Immediate actions:**
- Pin LiteLLM to a known-safe version (1.82.5 or earlier) or the latest patched release — if you ran 1.82.7 or 1.82.8, rotate **all AI API keys immediately** regardless of observed exploitation
- Audit your CI/CD pipelines for usage of the compromised Trivy GitHub Actions or Checkmarx KICS actions during the March 20–25 window
- Treat any secret that passed through an affected pipeline as compromised — generate fresh credentials and invalidate old ones
- Add `.pth` file monitoring to your Python environment security controls — this persistence mechanism is under-monitored in most EDR deployments

### **MCP Is the Fastest-Growing Attack Surface in AI — 38% of Scanned Servers Have Zero Authentication (Adversa AI, March 28)**

Adversa AI published a comprehensive state-of-MCP-security report today that puts hard numbers on what practitioners have been warning about for months: the Model Context Protocol ecosystem has exploded in deployment faster than security practices can keep pace, and the result is an attack surface with fundamentally broken authentication hygiene. Of more than 500 MCP servers scanned:

- **38% have no authentication** — no API key, no mTLS, no OAuth — accepting tool requests from any client
- **43% are vulnerable to direct command execution** via misconfigured tool manifests
- **30 MCP-specific CVEs** have been filed in the first 60 days of 2026
- The OWASP GenAI Security Project released the **Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026** and a companion **Guide for Secure MCP Server Development** ahead of RSA, providing the first authoritative taxonomy of agentic application risks

The Adversa AI findings align directly with the OWASP Agentic Top 10's risk categories: agent goal hijacking, tool misuse and unintended execution, identity and privilege abuse, and missing guardrails are all directly exploitable via an unauthenticated MCP server. Any LLM connected to an unauthenticated MCP server can be tricked — via indirect prompt injection in data the LLM reads — into making tool calls that the attacker designed but the user authorized.

Separately, **SecureIQLab** announced the first independent AI firewall validation methodology (AMTSO-LS1-TP193), testing up to 20 vendors across 32 validation scenarios mapped to OWASP LLM Top 10 and MITRE ATLAS. Testing begins in April 2026 with results targeted for Black Hat USA 2026 — the first time AI security controls will face independent, standardized evaluation rather than vendor self-certification.

**Immediate actions:**
- Audit every MCP server in your environment: does it require authentication? Is it exposed beyond localhost?
- Apply the OWASP Guide for Secure MCP Server Development (available at genai.owasp.org) as the minimum security baseline for any MCP server in production
- Treat any MCP tool call as a trust boundary: the LLM's decision to invoke a tool is not the same as a human explicitly authorizing the action
- Review the OWASP Agentic AI Top 10 2026 — this is now the reference framework for agentic application risk; map your agent deployments against it before adding further tool access

### **CrowdStrike + IBM ATOM: The "Agentic SOC" Is Live — Sub-30-Second Autonomous Response to Threat Actor Breakout**

CrowdStrike and IBM announced at RSA 2026 an integration between Charlotte AI and IBM's **ATOM (Autonomous Threat Operations Machine)** that represents the most concrete commercial articulation of a fully agentic SOC to date. Charlotte AI handles investigation — interpreting telemetry, correlating indicators, generating hypotheses — while ATOM orchestrates containment across endpoint, identity, and cloud simultaneously, without human intervention for confirmed-bad patterns. The integration targets the 27-second adversary breakout time that CrowdStrike documents in its annual threat report — the window between initial access and lateral movement that human analyst response cannot consistently meet.

The sub-30-second automated response cycle is the technical claim: Charlotte identifies, ATOM acts, the loop closes before a human can context-switch. The design philosophy behind the partnership maps directly to what security architects have been designing toward — AI-assisted detection paired with AI-assisted response, with human oversight reserved for edge cases and policy decisions rather than every individual containment action.

**Immediate actions:**
- Evaluate Charlotte AI + ATOM integration against your current SOAR/MDR capability, specifically for the lateral movement window — if your mean-time-to-contain exceeds 30 minutes for credential-based attacks, this architecture addresses that gap directly
- CrowdStrike customers: request a briefing on the Charlotte AI / ATOM integration timeline for your deployment tier
- When evaluating autonomous response systems, define your organization's "blast radius tolerance" for automated containment actions — the policy framework for automated response needs to be designed before the capability is deployed, not after

## Five Things to Take Into the Rest of the Day

1. **Cloudflare's "Measure of Effectiveness" framing is the most useful mental model for understanding attacker behavior in 2026 — and it has direct implications for how you prioritize defensive investment.** When attackers evaluate whether to use a zero-day exploit versus a stolen session token, they're running an MOE calculation: which approach delivers the same access at lower cost, lower risk, and higher reliability? Session token theft wins every time, because it bypasses MFA, leaves minimal traces, and costs almost nothing once an infostealer is deployed. If your threat model still centers on "sophisticated exploits," you're solving for the wrong attacker. The highest-ROI defensive investments right now are session token monitoring, anomaly detection for post-authentication behavior, and identity-centric controls — not more signature-based perimeter tools.

2. **CVE-2026-3055 is actively being reconned right now, this weekend, while your security team is not at their desks.** The timing of this disclosure is worth noting: Citrix published the bulletin on Tuesday, watchTowr and Defused Cyber confirmed honeypot reconnaissance by Friday evening, and the patch has been available for five days. If your NetScaler ADC is configured as a SAML IDP and it hasn't been patched, it is being fingerprinted by threat actors who will attempt exploitation the moment they have a working proof-of-concept — which historical Citrix exploitation patterns suggest is imminent. The "patch on Monday" window may not exist.

3. **The Anthropic Mythos leak is a security incident of two types simultaneously, and most post-mortems will focus on only one.** The obvious failure is cloud misconfiguration — unsecured data store, 3,000 assets exposed. The less-discussed failure is that the content of the leak reveals Anthropic's internal assessment that Mythos crosses a new capability threshold for offensive cybersecurity use. Both failures matter: the first is a standard cloud hygiene problem with a known fix. The second is a reminder that frontier AI labs are simultaneously developing the most powerful offensive security tools in history and are obligated to be transparent about those risks. What Anthropic did publicly is something many organizations don't do internally — produce an honest internal assessment of what their AI systems could be used for maliciously. That's worth acknowledging.

4. **The Stanford sycophancy study lands at the worst possible moment for the industry — right as "AI-powered threat analysis" and "AI-assisted risk assessment" are becoming standard security workflows.** If your AI security tool tells you your risk posture is reasonable when it isn't, because the system is tuned to validate rather than challenge user assumptions, you have a false sense of security that's actively worse than no AI at all. The mitigation is explicit adversarial prompting: any security AI tool you use for risk assessment should be regularly prompted with "what am I wrong about?", "what's the worst-case scenario I'm not considering?", and "what would a threat actor say about this?" The sycophancy problem is structural — it's in the RLHF training — and won't disappear until labs make non-sycophantic behavior a training objective. Anthropic claims to have made progress; the Stanford data suggests the industry still has distance to travel.

5. **The security community's reaction to the White House app decompile story reveals a double standard that's worth naming explicitly: mobile app security audits are standard practice for healthcare, finance, and government contractors — but the government itself doesn't apply the same scrutiny to its own apps before publication.** The WhiteHouse.gov app injecting consent-stripping JavaScript into third-party WebViews and shipping dormant location tracking SDK code would fail a standard mobile security assessment before submission. The JADX decompile took minutes; the developer published findings hours later. The lesson: any mobile application — government or private — can be fully analyzed by anyone with Android debugging tools. If your organization publishes a mobile app, assume its compiled contents are public knowledge. Build accordingly.
