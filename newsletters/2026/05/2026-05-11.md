# AI Security Newsletter: The Infrastructure of Trust
*Monday, May 11, 2026*

This week, every layer of the security stack is under negotiation. Threat actors have discovered that developer tools—note-taking apps, AI websites, plugin marketplaces—are easier to weaponize than the systems they were designed to complement. Defenders are learning that AI vulnerability scanners are genuinely useful but not magical. And the community is asking a harder question: who controls the infrastructure that decides what software is trusted at all? Today's packet follows that thread from the plugin level to the hardware attestation layer, with stops at a blockchain-based RAT, an honest post-mortem on an AI model's hyped scanning capabilities, and fresh research on why your AI red-team agent has a stubborn blind spot it won't admit to.

## 1. Headliners

### PHANTOMPULSE RAT Weaponizes Obsidian Vaults in Targeted Financial Campaign
Security researchers have documented a sophisticated social engineering campaign — designated REF6598 — targeting professionals in finance and cryptocurrency. The attack does not begin with a phishing email. It begins with a LinkedIn message from someone who appears to be a venture capitalist.

**The Attack Chain:**
After establishing trust over days or weeks via LinkedIn and Telegram, the attacker invites the target to collaborate on a shared, cloud-hosted Obsidian vault. The vault looks legitimate. The infection is triggered only when the victim enables "Installed community plugins" synchronization — a manual action that requires deliberate user approval, making it feel low-risk. That approval silently activates malicious versions of the widely-used 'Shell Commands' and 'Hider' plugins embedded in the vault.

On Windows, the plugins execute a PowerShell stager that drops PHANTOMPULL, a loader that decrypts and injects the final payload — PHANTOMPULSE — directly into memory, bypassing file-based detection. On macOS, AppleScript handles the equivalent delivery.

**The C2 Innovation:**
PHANTOMPULSE resolves its command-and-control server address by querying the Ethereum blockchain. The IP address is embedded in the data of the most recent transaction from a hardcoded wallet. This is operationally significant: there is no DNS record to sinkhole, no IP block that kills the campaign. The C2 infrastructure lives on a decentralized ledger that no incident responder can take down.

Once running, PHANTOMPULSE captures keystrokes, takes screenshots, exfiltrates files, and executes arbitrary commands. For a cryptocurrency professional, a full compromise means wallet keys, exchange credentials, trading strategies, and corporate deal flow — all extracted silently.

**Detection Indicators:**
- `Obsidian.exe` or `Obsidian.app` spawning child processes: `powershell.exe`, `cmd.exe`, `bash`, `osascript` — highly anomalous
- PowerShell execution with `-ExecutionPolicy Bypass` initiated by a non-standard parent process
- Outbound connections from unexpected processes to Ethereum blockchain nodes or gateways
- New or modified files under `[VaultPath]/.obsidian/plugins/` outside of the official Obsidian marketplace

**Why it matters:** REF6598 represents a meaningful evolution in attack sophistication: the infection vector is a trusted collaboration tool, the initial access technique requires no exploit (only social engineering), and the C2 infrastructure is effectively censorship-resistant. Every organization whose employees use Obsidian — and particularly those in finance and crypto — should add Obsidian plugin monitoring to their EDR rules today.

*Source: [NetSecOps Cyber Intelligence](https://cyber.netsecops.io/articles/obsidian-plugin-abused-in-campaign-to-deploy-phantom-pulse-rat/)*

### Anthropic Mythos Scanned 176,000 Lines of curl. It Found One Real CVE.
In April 2026, Anthropic announced Project Glasswing — a defensive cybersecurity initiative involving AWS, Apple, Google, Microsoft, NVIDIA, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, and others — backed by a commitment of $100M in credits for Claude Mythos Preview, a frontier model that Anthropic described as capable of surpassing "all but the most skilled humans" at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities. The announcement triggered significant media anxiety.

Now we have a real-world data point.

Daniel Stenberg, the lead developer of curl, received a Mythos-generated analysis of curl's 176,000-line codebase (660,000 words — 12% longer than *War and Peace*). The model analyzed 178,000 lines in the `src/` and `lib/` directories and returned 5 "confirmed security vulnerabilities." After the curl security team investigated, they found:

- **1 confirmed real vulnerability** — a low-severity CVE, details embargoed until the curl 8.21.0 release in late June
- **3 false positives** — findings that described documented API behavior, not vulnerabilities
- **1 "just a bug"** — not a security issue

Stenberg's conclusion is worth quoting directly: *"My personal conclusion can however not end up with anything else than that the big hype around this model so far was primarily marketing. I see no evidence that this setup finds issues to any particular higher or more advanced degree than the other tools have done before Mythos."*

He is careful to note that Mythos still contributed value — about 20 total bugs described with high explanatory quality, few false positives, high signal-to-noise — and that the single low-severity CVE was not previously found by AISLE, Zeropath, or OpenAI Codex Security, which had already collectively triggered 200-300 bugfixes in curl over the prior 8-10 months.

**The strategic read:** Mythos appears to be a genuinely good AI code analyzer operating at the frontier of a field where all modern AI models are already "significantly better than any traditional code analyzers." Project Glasswing's value is not that Mythos found a devastating zero-day in curl — it's that Anthropic has organized a coordinated defensive response to a capability that will proliferate regardless, and is routing it toward hardening open-source infrastructure before attackers use the same capability offensively.

Stenberg closes with a point that deserves attention: *"Not using AI code analyzers in your project means that you leave adversaries and attackers time and opportunity to find and exploit the flaws you don't find."*

*Source: [Daniel Stenberg's Blog](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/05/11/mythos-finds-a-curl-vulnerability/) | [Anthropic Project Glasswing](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing)*

## 2. Breaking News

### Fake Claude AI Website Delivers Beagle Windows Backdoor (PlugX Attribution)
**Published: May 7, 2026**

Sophos has published technical details on `claude-pro[.]com`, a typosquatted site impersonating Anthropic's Claude that delivers a previously undocumented Windows backdoor named Beagle. The malicious download — a 505MB archive named `Claude-Pro-windows-x64.zip` — contains an MSI installer for what it calls "Claude-Pro Relay," a supposed high-performance relay for Claude Code developers.

The infection chain uses G Data's legitimately signed `NOVupdate.exe` binary to sideload a malicious `avk.dll`, which decrypts and executes DonutLoader from `NOVupdate.exe.dat` in memory. DonutLoader then deploys Beagle — a lean backdoor with eight commands (`uninstall`, `cmd`, `upload`, `download`, `mkdir`, `rename`, `ls`, `rm`) — communicating via AES-encrypted channels over TCP port 443 and UDP port 8080 to C2 infrastructure at an Alibaba Cloud-hosted IP.

The DLL sideloading technique using a G Data signed binary has been previously linked to PlugX operations, suggesting state-affiliated actors are adapting their TTPs to exploit AI brand trust. Additional Beagle samples on VirusTotal (February–April 2026) show the same XOR key being used in campaigns impersonating CrowdStrike, SentinelOne, and Trellix update sites.

**Mitigation:** Download Claude only from `claude.ai` or `anthropic.com`. Presence of `NOVupdate.exe` outside the G Data installation directory is a strong compromise indicator.

*Source: [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fake-claude-ai-website-delivers-new-beagle-windows-malware/) | [Sophos Research](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/blog/donuts-and-beagles-fake-claude-site-spreads-backdoor)*

### AI Meeting Notetakers Are Creating Legal Liability for Law Firms
**Published: May 9, 2026**

The legal industry is grappling with an emerging governance problem: AI notetaking tools (Otter, Fireflies, Grain, and others) routinely record, transcribe, and cloud-store meeting content — including conversations between attorneys and clients. The core concern is attorney-client privilege: recordings stored on third-party servers may be accessible via subpoena, government request, or breach. At least three major law firms have now issued internal policies restricting or banning AI meeting recorders in client-facing settings.

The legal risk calculus is clear: the convenience of automated transcription does not outweigh the discovery risk and privilege waiver exposure when sensitive litigation strategy is captured and uploaded to an AI vendor's cloud. The same dynamics apply in any regulated industry — healthcare, finance, defense contracting — where sensitive conversations occur in meeting settings that developers reflexively equip with "smart" recording tools.

*Source: [The New York Times / DealBook](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/09/business/dealbook/ai-notetakers-legal-risk.html)*

## 3. Technical Deep Dives

### CyBiasBench: AI Offensive Agents Have Persistent Attack-Family Blind Spots
*arXiv:2605.07830 | Published May 8, 2026 | Under Review*

A new benchmark paper introduces CyBiasBench — 630 sessions evaluating five LLM agents across three targets, four prompt conditions, and ten attack families — and documents a phenomenon the authors call **attack-selection bias**.

**The finding:** Every tested LLM agent disproportionately concentrates its attack efforts on a narrow subset of attack families, regardless of prompt variations. This bias is better characterized as an intrinsic trait of the agent (not a prompt artifact), as measured by the entropy of each agent's attack-family allocation distribution. Different agents show different dominant attack families — meaning no two agents share the same blind spots.

**Bias momentum:** When researchers explicitly steered agents toward unfamiliar attack families via prompting, agents resisted. The forced distribution shift produced no measurable improvement in attack success rate. The agent preferred its familiar territory even when ordered otherwise.

**Why this matters for defenders:**
Organizations using AI agents for penetration testing or red-teaming face a systematic coverage problem. A single AI red-team agent will miss attack families it is biased against — not because of capability limitations, but because of deeply embedded selection tendencies. The practical implication is that **AI red-team operations require agent ensembles** with diverse bias profiles, not a single "best" model deployed autonomously.

This also has implications for AI-powered attack detection. If an organization has threat-modeled based on the attack patterns an AI agent preferentially selects, they may be under-defended against less "fashionable" attack families that human adversaries — who do not share these biases — will exploit.

### CodeQL's Blind Spots: SAST Version Updates Can Un-Detect Known CVEs
*arXiv:2605.07900 | Published May 11, 2026*

The largest academic longitudinal study of a SAST tool to date: 114 versions of CodeQL evaluated against 3,993 CVEs from 1,622 repositories — more than 20 billion lines of code analyzed.

**Key findings:**
- CodeQL identified 171 total CVEs across all versions
- For 83 of those, at least one CodeQL version *prior* to the fix detected the vulnerability — meaning it was findable before patching
- **21 CVEs were no longer detected after a CodeQL version update** — and 17 of those were never redetected in any subsequent version
- Detection is "non-monotonic": a tool upgrade can silently eliminate coverage for previously-detected vulnerabilities

**The operational risk:** Organizations that rely on CodeQL (or any SAST tool) as a continuous security gate face an invisible risk: a routine tool version bump may eliminate detection of CVEs that were previously flagged. This creates the appearance of a clean scan while genuine vulnerabilities remain.

**The AI compound risk:** As teams layer AI-assisted code review on top of SAST, they may assume the combination provides comprehensive coverage. The reality is that each layer has distinct, version-dependent blind spots. The only mitigation is longitudinal tracking of SAST detections across tool versions and regular re-scanning of historical codebases with updated tools.

*Source: [arXiv:2605.07900](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07900)*

## 4. Community Pulse

### Hardware Attestation as Monopoly: The Gating Layer Below AI
*1,713 points on Hacker News — top story of the day*

GrapheneOS published a detailed thread arguing that Apple and Google are expanding the scope of hardware attestation requirements in ways that functionally block alternative operating systems from accessing attestation-gated APIs. Banking apps, government services, and increasingly enterprise MDM systems require attestation to verify "trusted" OS state. On Android, this effectively means stock or lightly modified OEM ROMs pass; privacy-hardened alternatives like GrapheneOS do not — despite GrapheneOS being objectively more secure.

The thread is generating significant discussion because the pattern applies directly to AI security: as on-device AI inference scales, attestation will become the gating mechanism that determines what models, what runtimes, and what applications are "trusted" on a device. Vendors who control the attestation root of trust will have the ability to block AI software that competes with their own, or that does not meet their data-access requirements.

**The strategic implication for AI security:** Hardware attestation is rapidly becoming the enforcement layer for AI governance at the device level. Security teams should monitor how attestation requirements expand in enterprise MDM policy — and whether AI applications are moving toward attestation-gated model access.

### "Local AI Needs to Be the Norm" — The Privacy Case Against Cloud-Dependent Features
*1,297 points on Hacker News*

A widely shared essay from unix.foo makes a practical argument that has direct enterprise security implications: shipping cloud AI API calls inside local applications creates an unnecessary, frequently under-examined dependency that exposes user data to third-party retention, breach risk, and government request.

The author builds the case using Apple's `FoundationModels` framework as a concrete counterexample — showing that summarization features built on on-device inference require no server round-trip, no user consent for data retention, no exposure to vendor outages, and no billing infrastructure. The Silicon in modern devices has dedicated Neural Engines sitting idle while apps wait for JSON from remote inference servers.

**The governance translation:** Security and compliance teams approving AI-enabled tools should now explicitly evaluate *where inference happens*. Tools that perform inference on-device inherit none of the third-party data-handling risks that cloud inference tools carry. "Does inference happen on-device or on a third-party server?" should be a standard due diligence question alongside "what data does this tool store?"

*Source: [unix.foo](https://unix.foo/posts/local-ai-needs-to-be-norm/) | [HN Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48085821)*

## 5. The Daily Feed

**AI Coding Agents and the Maintenance Debt Time Bomb**
James Shore's essay, surfaced on HN with 209 points, makes a rigorous argument that AI coding velocity is only valuable if it does not proportionally increase maintenance costs. Using a crowd-sourced maintenance cost model, Shore shows that doubling code output while doubling maintenance burden produces no net productivity gain after ~2.5 years, and negative gains thereafter. Security implication: AI-generated code that is harder to understand and review — which is common — creates a security debt that compounds. "Confident AI is not Correct AI" extends here: agentic tools that ship code faster than it can be audited are building a deferred security risk.
*Source: [jamesshore.com](https://www.jamesshore.com/v2/blog/2026/you-need-ai-that-reduces-your-maintenance-costs) | [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48089289)*

**Security Design DSL Gap — Are Your Design and Code Even Talking to Each Other?**
A new arXiv paper (2605.07814, May 8, 2026) studied 66 security design DSLs and 559 security checks from 36 code analyzers, validated by 22 security experts. Finding: there is a fundamental disconnect between what security architects express in design-level DSLs and what code-level analyzers actually check. Security checks are described in overly general weaknesses (CWEs) that don't map cleanly to design-level concepts. Even experts struggled to navigate this gap. Practical implication: organizations that believe their threat model is "implemented" in their SAST configuration are likely wrong. The design-to-code security traceability problem is unsolved.

## 6. Five Things to Take Into the Rest of the Day

1. **Add Obsidian plugin spawning to your EDR rules — now.** The PHANTOMPULSE campaign shows that any application with a plugin system that can execute shell commands is an infection vector. If `Obsidian.exe` or `Obsidian.app` spawns `powershell.exe`, `cmd.exe`, or `osascript` on a workstation, that's a detection you want. The same logic applies to Logseq, Obsidian, Cursor, and any other tool that supports community plugins with shell access.

2. **Project Glasswing's real value is coordination, not magic.** Mythos finding one low-severity CVE in one of the most audited codebases on earth is actually the correct result. The important story is that Anthropic has organized 40+ critical infrastructure owners to defensively scan before the same capability proliferates to adversaries. Understand Glasswing as a head-start program, not a vulnerability oracle.

3. **AI red-team agents have stubborn coverage blind spots — ensemble them.** CyBiasBench's finding that LLM agents have persistent attack-family biases that resist prompting means you cannot fix this with better instructions. If you are using AI for adversarial simulation, you need multiple agents with diverse profiles — or you are systematically missing attack families your human adversaries are not biased against.

4. **SAST upgrades can silently un-detect CVEs.** Before updating your CodeQL or any other SAST version, run the new version against the same baseline as the previous one and diff the results. A version upgrade that removes 17 previously-detected CVE patterns without announcement is a real operational risk, and the arXiv:2605.07900 study confirms this happens in production.

5. **Audit AI tool inference location as a governance checkpoint.** The `unix.foo` essay and the growing legal concern about AI notetakers both point to the same governance gap: organizations are approving AI-enabled tools without asking where inference happens. Build "on-device vs. cloud inference" into your AI tool evaluation checklist. Tools that perform inference locally inherit none of the third-party data retention and breach risks of cloud-inference tools — and that difference matters for compliance, attorney-client privilege, and regulatory exposure.
