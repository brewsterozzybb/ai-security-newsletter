# AI Security Newsletter — August 26, 2026

---

## Headliners
The local AI agent just joined the browser's attack surface. Cyera published the first widely-reported drive-by takeover of a local LLM agent (NVIDIA NemoClaw, CVE-2026-65105), and the same week Dream Research Labs walked back the curtain on a near-autonomous intrusion against an Asian government in which open-source agent frameworks, not bespoke malware, did almost all of the work. Both are the same story told from opposite ends: the attacker who never has to touch your box because your developer already installed a convenience wrapper, and the attacker who is just an LLM with a notebook and an unbounded HTTP client. Gartner's 2Q26 emerging-risks survey now puts AI vulnerability discovery at the top of the list — moved from outside the top five to #1 in three months — which means the discovery asymmetry is no longer a research curiosity, it is a budget line.

## Breaking News

### NemoClaw Hands Drive-By Attackers a Persistent Backdoor Into the Local LLM
Cyera disclosed [CVE-2026-65105](https://www.cyera.com/research/nemoclaw-one-website-visit-to-hijack-your-ai-agent) on August 25, a vulnerability in NVIDIA's NemoClaw deployment wrapper that lets a single visit to a malicious webpage give an attacker unauthenticated, persistent control over a developer's local AI agent. The root cause is not exotic: NemoClaw configures its local Ollama inference backend with `OLLAMA_HOST=0.0.0.0:11434` so the OpenShell sandbox container can reach it, which incidentally disables Ollama's Host-header validation. Pair that with DNS rebinding from any tab the developer has open and the browser's same-origin model routes attacker JavaScript straight to Ollama's API. The crown-jewel primitive is **chat-template poisoning**: an attacker rewrites the model template, which means the malicious instructions survive every system prompt the agent writes from that point forward — including reboots and model swaps.

Key findings:
- **Attack chain length is zero clicks** — DNS rebinding from a tab the developer already has open. No prompt required from the user.
- **Persistence is on the model, not the process** — template poisoning survives a fresh chat, a relaunched agent, and most "clear context" affordances.
- **Primitive grants the full Ollama surface** — model enumeration, deletion, GPU exhaustion via infinite inference, arbitrary model download (disk fill), and the template-rewrite itself.
- **Researcher attribution** — Elad Luz and Ofek Itach at Oasis Security; responsible disclosure to NVIDIA PSIRT before publication.

**Immediate actions:**
- Identify every host running NemoClaw / OpenClaw / OpenShell in your fleet and confirm whether the embedded Ollama is bound to `0.0.0.0`. Bind it to `127.0.0.1` and require a reverse proxy with auth in front of any cross-container use.
- Add `*.openclaw.ai` and your NemoClaw management hostnames to your DNS-pinning / browser-rebinding defenses (Chrome's `Private Network Access` policies, `disable_non_proxied_udp` analogues, or a corp DNS that strips 0.0.0.0 rebinding answers).
- Audit installed Ollama models for unexpected modifications to the chat-template field (`/api/show`); template drift is the indicator, not the network connection. Coverage from [Cybersecurity News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/nvidia-nemoclaw-flaw/) and [GBHackers](https://gbhackers.com/nvidia-nemoclaw-vulnerability/) for corroboration.

### Open-Source Agent Frameworks Carried a Government Intrusion End-to-End
Dream Research Labs published operational artifacts on August 25 from a near-autonomous intrusion against government systems in Asia (public reporting has identified Taiwan) carried out by an actor using **Hermes** and **OpenClaw** as their offensive stack. The campaign ran 12 attack waves over four days (July 1–4, 2026) with up to eight sub-agents working in parallel, prioritizing 14 attack chains through Bayesian scoring, refreshing its own playbooks through "Learning Cycles" against CVE databases and GitHub, and solving CAPTCHAs with Tesseract OCR. The most uncomfortable number in the report: **84 of 85 cracked accounts successfully authenticated through the target's SSO bridge — a 98.8% reuse rate** — because the framework had already extracted a full employee database from an unauthenticated API and harvested OAuth client IDs and Keycloak configuration by decompiling the portal's JavaScript bundle. Reporting at [Cybersecurity News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/eight-ai-agents-breach-government-systems/) and [GBHackers](https://gbhackers.com/multi-agent-ai-framework-compromises-government-systems/) corroborates the details; the primary report (Dream Research Labs) was not directly retrievable in this run, so treat specifics as cross-checked but second-hand until the original drops.

Key findings:
- **JWT `alg: none` accepted in production** — agents discovered a token-validation flaw on a connected government app and used it to forge authenticated sessions at will.
- **Recon was fully agentic** — decompiling Angular JS bundles, mapping 21 connected systems and 6 SSO sub-realms, harvesting RSA keys, and finding a 36-endpoint API surface, unauthenticated in places.
- **98.8% SSO reuse** — once the agent cracked credentials, the SSO bridge trusted them; the AI did not need to do anything clever after authentication.
- **2,564 personnel records exfiltrated** — 1,409 employees + 916 from an unauthenticated API + 239 legal-professional records, per the report summary.

**Immediate actions:**
- Inventory every public-facing government or B2B portal you operate. Run a one-page hostile-recon test: can a fresh account, an unverified email, and a default password reach an employee list, an SSO discovery endpoint, or a JWT validation step that doesn't check the algorithm?
- Stop trusting `alg: none` in any JWT library older than your last dependency review; verify your gateway and identity provider both reject unverified signatures regardless of header claim.
- Treat the existence of an Angular / SPA frontend with embedded OAuth client IDs and Keycloak config as reconnaissance already public. Move to a backend-for-frontend pattern where the secrets live server-side and the SPA only gets short-lived, scope-narrow tokens.

## Technical Deep Dives

### "Trusted Data Path" Is the New Shell: Gitea and Marimo Both Flunked This Week
Two CVEs in adjacent products this week share the same failure shape, and that shape is what your AI coding agent is going to inherit if you don't break it up. CISA added [CVE-2026-60004](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/08/25/cisa-adds-one-known-exploited-vulnerability-catalog) to the KEV catalog on August 25 — Gitea's diffpatch endpoint installs and executes a Git hook from repository-controlled content (CVSS 9.8). With default-open registration, an unauthenticated visitor registers, creates a repository, and triggers RCE as the Gitea OS user; [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/08/26/gitea-cve-2026-60004-exploited-in-the-wild/) and [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/08/critical-gitea-rce-actively-exploited.html) confirm active exploitation with a real miner-style payload. The same day, [CVE-2026-75149](https://www.it-boltwise.de/marimo-schliesst-mcp-code-injection-cve-2026-75149-im-edit-modus.html) (CVSS v4 8.7) — a code injection in Marimo notebooks reachable through MCP-driven configuration when a notebook is opened in edit mode — was published with a fix in 0.23.15. The pattern is not subtle: a component trusted to consume structured input (a patch, a notebook config) accepted attacker-controlled data and turned it into a local subprocess. Your Claude Code or Codex agent is in the same trust tier as the Gitea diffpatch handler: it consumes a repository or a notebook as input, and its runtime executes whatever it finds. CVE-2026-60004 is what happens when the runtime does not have a human in the loop, and CVE-2026-75149 is what happens when the runtime has an MCP bridge it didn't write.

**Immediate actions:**
- Patch self-hosted Gitea to 1.27.1 today; the CISA KEV binding operational directive has a 14-day clock for federal agencies and you should treat that as your SLA even if you are private sector.
- If you run an AI agent on top of Gitea (PR review bots, code-search copilots, auto-PR fixers), assume the Gitea service account is a hostile-code executor and isolate it: separate UID, no `docker.sock`, network egress filtered, and a write-only logging sidecar so a successful exploit can't rewrite its own audit trail.
- For Marimo: confirm version is ≥ 0.23.15. For every other "notebook as data" surface you trust your agents to ingest (Jupyter, Observable, Hex, Deepnote), apply the same read-only-fs and no-shell pattern until you can show otherwise.

### The Discovery Backlog Just Outran Your Patch SLA — and a Trust-Cascade Model Explains Why
Two reports landed this week that, read together, explain why the Gitea and Marimo stories will keep coming. Gartner's 2Q26 Emerging Risks Monitor — summarized by [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/08/26/ai-vulnerability-discovery-emerging-risks/) — moved AI vulnerability discovery from outside the top five in 1Q26 to **#1 in 2Q26** across 316 senior risk, audit, and executive respondents, with 78% of BFSI respondents putting it in their top ten and a time-frame score of 1.92 (impact within 1–2 years). The reframing in the report is the bit to internalize: "the step from finding a flaw to holding working attack code has shrunk to close to nothing." That is exactly what the Dream/Taiwan campaign demonstrates operationally. CYFIRMA's [Trust Cascade](https://www.hendryadrian.com/the-trust-cascade/) report (deep-dive summary) composes the same week into a four-case pattern — Vercel/Context.ai (infostealer → OAuth grant → lateral), Salesloft/Drift (vendor token → 700 organizations), the Taiwan AI-assisted intrusion, and EchoLeak (Copilot prompt injection without identity compromise) — and identifies task-scoped authorization, SaaS-to-SaaS trust-graph visibility, and non-human identity governance as the highest-leverage controls. The takeaway for a practitioner: the bottleneck has moved from "can the attacker find a vuln" to "can the attacker reuse one trust relationship" — and that second question is exactly what your agentic attack chain exploits in seconds.

**Immediate actions:**
- Recalibrate your CVE-patch SLA. If your prior SLA assumed "researcher disclosure → 90 days of public patch window," assume a frontier model writes working exploit code the same week the advisory drops. Federal CISA BOD timelines are a reasonable floor, not a ceiling.
- Draw your non-human identity map. List every service account, every OAuth grant, every MCP bridge, every AI agent token, and every vendor's token you forward to a sub-processor. If you cannot list them, you cannot revoke them when one trust relationship cascades.
- Adopt task-scoped authorization on your AI agents now, even as a manual process. Every tool call gets a scope; every scope has a max duration; every scope is logged to a place the agent cannot write to. Don't wait for CrowdStrike-style Continuous Identity to mature; the Dream campaign ran in four days.

## Five Things to Take Into the Rest of the Day
1. **Your "local" AI agent is not local in any security-relevant sense.** NemoClaw bound Ollama to 0.0.0.0 so the OpenShell container could reach it; Ollama then turned off Host-header validation; a browser tab then DNS-rebound to it. Every convenience wrapper you install to make local inference "just work" is a new egress point you didn't draw on your network diagram. Draw it now, and treat any AI-tooling port that listens on 0.0.0.0 as an exposure until proven otherwise. If your team shipped a "local RAG," an "agentic IDE plugin," or a "sidecar Ollama" this quarter, assume it is internet-reachable from any browser tab on the host until you can show the binding.

2. **Discovery asymmetry has officially overtaken your patch SLA.** Gartner's 2Q26 survey put AI vulnerability discovery at #1; the share of BFSI respondents ranking it top-10 is 78%, and the average time-frame score puts the impact inside two years. The Dream/Taiwan campaign used an LLM to write the recon code, the OCR bypass, and the JWT forge in four days. The bottleneck is no longer "can the attacker find a vuln" — it is "can the defender act in the same window the model does." If your patch cadence assumes human-speed exploitation, your assumptions are now demonstrably wrong.

3. **The Dream campaign is a better brief than any vendor whitepaper.** Eight sub-agents, 12 attack waves, four days, 98.8% SSO reuse after cracking credentials, 2,564 records out, no custom malware. Print the two Cybersecurity News / GBHackers write-ups, hand them to your detection engineering team, and ask: "what would our EDR / IdP / WAF logs have caught?" If the answer is "we'd see the authentication events but not the agent's reasoning," you are in the same position as the Taiwan target before July 1.

4. **Open-weight frontier models are arriving faster than your threat model can be updated.** Ox Alpha — the new Z.AI GLM model with weights dropping tonight — joined OpenRouter's leaderboard a week before the lab confirmed what it was. By the time your team formally evaluates a new model, your developers have probably already pointed an agent at it. The defensive move is not to block the models; it is to instrument the agent-to-model boundary so that prompts, tool calls, and tool outputs are captured to a place the model cannot rewrite.

5. **The "trust cascade" is the model your IR playbooks need now.** CYFIRMA's composition of Vercel/Context.ai, Salesloft/Drift, the Taiwan intrusion, and EchoLeak shows that the question "was the identity layer compromised?" is no longer binary — it is a graph. The Taiwan case was not identity-first but used identity trust (SSO) at the end; EchoLeak had no identity compromise at all. If your incident playbook still asks "how did they get in?" as the first question, rewrite it to ask "what trust relationships did they chain, and which one did we not revoke in time?" Revocation is the control; logging the chain is what makes revocation possible.
